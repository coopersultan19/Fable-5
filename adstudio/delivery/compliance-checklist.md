# Pre-Delivery Compliance Checklist — Supplement Ad Scripts

Run **every** script through this before delivery. No exceptions, including free
samples — the free sample is the sales pitch for our compliance pillar, so it has
to be the cleanest ad in the batch.

> **Scope note:** This is operational guidance for scriptwriting, not legal
> advice. We are a creative studio. The client owns final claim substantiation
> and legal review. If a script feels borderline after this checklist, rewrite
> it — there is always a compliant way to say the interesting thing.

---

## 1. The core rule

Dietary supplements may make **structure/function claims** (how a nutrient
affects the normal structure or function of the body) but never **disease
claims** (diagnose, treat, cure, mitigate, or prevent any disease or symptom of
a disease). The line is not about being "natural-sounding" — it's about whether
the sentence implies a disease state.

**Quick test for any line in a script:** *"Does this sentence name, imply, or
promise to fix a medical condition?"* If yes, rewrite it.

---

## 2. Allowed patterns — structure/function claims (10 examples)

| # | Allowed | Why it's fine |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Supports muscle strength and recovery" | Normal body function, no condition |
| 2 | "Promotes restful sleep" | Function of a healthy body |
| 3 | "Helps maintain healthy digestion" | Maintenance of normal function |
| 4 | "Supports a calm, balanced mood" | Normal state, not a disorder |
| 5 | "Helps reduce occasional bloating after meals" | "Occasional" keeps it non-disease |
| 6 | "Supports your body's natural energy production" | Normal metabolic function |
| 7 | "Helps maintain hormonal balance throughout your cycle" | Normal physiological process |
| 8 | "Supports immune system function" | General function, no illness named |
| 9 | "Promotes healthy skin, hair, and nails" | Normal structure claim |
| 10 | "Helps you fall asleep faster and wake up refreshed"* | Sleep quality framing, not insomnia — *keep it tied to occasional sleeplessness, never a chronic problem |

**Load-bearing verbs: supports, promotes, helps maintain, contributes to.**
**Load-bearing qualifiers: occasional, everyday, normal, healthy.**

---

## 3. Prohibited patterns — disease claims (10 examples)

| # | Prohibited | Why it fails | Compliant rewrite |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Cures insomnia" | Names a disease + "cures" | "Promotes restful sleep" |
| 2 | "Treats anxiety and depression" | Named disorders | "Supports a calm, balanced mood" |
| 3 | "Reduces inflammation" | Implied disease state (chronic inflammation) | "Supports joint comfort after exercise" |
| 4 | "Fixes your gut / heals leaky gut" | Disease/repair claim | "Supports gut health and comfortable digestion" |
| 5 | "Prevents colds and flu" | Disease prevention | "Supports immune function, especially during busy seasons" |
| 6 | "Balances your hormones to eliminate PMS symptoms" | PMS symptom elimination = disease claim | "Supports hormonal balance and comfort throughout your cycle" |
| 7 | "Lowers blood pressure / cholesterol" | Medical biomarkers = drug claim | Cut it. There is no compliant rewrite for this one |
| 8 | "Better than antidepressants / replaces your meds" | Drug comparison + implied treatment | Cut it entirely — never reference medications |
| 9 | "Melts fat / clinically proven to cause 10 lbs of weight loss" | Disease-adjacent + unsubstantiated "clinically proven" | "Supports your metabolism alongside diet and exercise" |
| 10 | "Doctors don't want you to know about this cure" | "Cure" + conspiracy framing (Meta insta-flag) | Cut it. This framing is banned on every platform |

**Kill-on-sight words: cure, treat, heal, fix, prevent [disease], reverse,
eliminate, clinically proven (without an actual clinical trial on the finished
product), anti-inflammatory, prescription, medication.**

---

## 4. Required disclaimers

- **FDA disclaimer** — if the ad or landing page makes structure/function
  claims, the client's page must carry: *"These statements have not been
  evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to
  diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease."* We don't control their site,
  but we flag it in the delivery note if their product page is missing it.
- **FTC material connection** — any script written in testimonial voice ("I've
  been taking this for 3 weeks…") must be deliverable with a clear disclosure.
  Include `#ad` in our suggested caption text for every testimonial-style ad.
  Scripted portrayals must never claim to be an actual customer's experience.
- **Results language** — no "results in X days" promises without substantiation.
  Use "everyone's different" framing or omit timelines.
- **No implied endorsements** — no white coats, no "doctor recommended," no
  fake news-anchor framing, no before/after photos in scripts we write.

## 5. Meta & TikTok supplement landmines

These get ads rejected or accounts flagged even when the FDA/FTC line is clean:

1. **Personal attributes rule (Meta):** never call out the viewer's condition —
   "struggling with bloating?" directly addresses a personal health state.
   Rewrite third-person: "bloating after meals is more common than people admit."
2. **Before/after imagery:** banned territory on both platforms for health
   products. Never script visual before/after transformations or "week 1 vs
   week 4" body shots.
3. **Negative self-image (TikTok especially):** no body-shaming hooks, no
   "hate how you look?" framing. TikTok rejects these fast on supplement ads.
4. **Weight-loss claims:** the most-flagged supplement category on both
   platforms. Metabolism/energy framing only, and never target under-18s.
5. **"Miracle" language:** miracle, magic, breakthrough, secret — pattern-matched
   by both platforms' review systems. Also, it's tacky.
6. **Sensational health claims in the first 3 seconds:** review systems weight
   the opening. Keep the hook about the *person and situation*, not the claim —
   claims live mid-ad in supports/promotes language.
7. **Comment-bait exaggeration:** "this literally changed my life overnight" in
   VO or captions reads as an unrealistic result claim.
8. **Ingredient ≠ product claims:** a study about magnesium is not a claim your
   client's gummy can borrow ("studies show THIS product…"). Script ingredient
   education generically: "magnesium plays a role in over 300 processes in the
   body."

---

## 6. The pre-delivery gate (run per ad)

- [ ] Every claim uses supports / promotes / helps maintain framing
- [ ] Zero disease names, symptoms, or implied medical conditions
- [ ] Zero kill-list words (cure, treat, heal, prevent, reverse, clinically proven, anti-inflammatory)
- [ ] No medication references or comparisons
- [ ] Testimonial-voice ads carry `#ad` in suggested caption; no fake "real customer" claims
- [ ] No second-person condition call-outs ("do YOU suffer from…")
- [ ] No before/after visuals or body-transformation framing
- [ ] No results timelines without substantiation
- [ ] Hook (first 3 s) is situational, not claim-heavy
- [ ] Client's product page has the FDA disclaimer (flag in delivery note if missing)
- [ ] Gut check: would I be comfortable if Meta's policy team watched this ad next to the client's product page?

**If any box fails, the ad does not ship.** Rewrite is always cheaper than a
client's banned ad account — and "we caught this before Meta did" is the best
retention message we have.
